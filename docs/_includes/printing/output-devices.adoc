Where a document is going to be printed decides how to print it.  A desktop inkjet or laser
printer wants a composite job through its own driver, color-managed by ColorSync, and gets it
with no settings at all.  A commercial press wants separated, marked, bled plates -- or, more
often today, a PDF the shop's prepress system will separate itself.  This page explains what
{product} sends to each kind of device, which options matter for which, and how to get a job
to a service provider.

NOTE: There is no PPD to choose and no PostScript file to write.  macOS talks to every printer
through its queue; {product} draws every job as PDF.  Separations are produced by {product}
itself rather than by the printer's PostScript interpreter, so they work on any device --
including a PDF file.

== Desktop printers and proofs

For a proof or a black-and-white check, any printer on your Mac will do:

. Choose menu:File[Print…] and pick the printer.
. Leave *Composite* selected in the *{product}* pane.
. Set *Fit on paper* if the page is larger than the paper.
. Click btn:[Print].

Colors go to the printer as the document defines them and ColorSync converts them with the
printer's profile chosen in the *Color Matching* pane (xref:color-management.adoc[Color
management]).  Spot colors print as their closest process or RGB equivalent.  Overprinting
objects print as they look with menu:View[Overprint Preview] on.

If a complex document prints slowly or not at all, select *Rasterize output* in the *Imaging*
group and choose a resolution: the whole sheet is sent as one image, which every printer can
handle (xref:print-performance.adoc[Faster printing]).

== High-resolution output and separations

A platesetter, an imagesetter making film, or a proofing system driven by a prepress RIP
usually wants one of two things: a composite PDF the RIP separates and screens, or finished
separations.  Ask the shop which.

To produce a composite PDF for a prepress RIP, use xref:export-pdf.adoc[Exporting PDF] with a
PDF/X preset; it embeds profiles, keeps spot colors as spot, and writes trim and bleed boxes
the RIP reads.  The Print dialog's *Save as PDF* also works but writes a plain PDF.

To produce separations yourself:

. Choose menu:File[Print…] and open the *{product}* pane.
. Under *Output*, select *Separations*.
. In the ink list, clear *Print* for any plate you do not want.  Select *Print spot colors as
  process* if the job is four-color only.
. Set the screen *Angle* and *Frequency* per plate if the shop gave you values, and choose the
  dot shape under *Halftone screen*.
. Turn on the *Crop marks*, *Registration marks* and *Separation names* the shop asked for, and
  set the *Bleed*.
. Under *Imaging*, set *Emulsion* and *Positive* or *Negative* as instructed for film.
. Decide who screens the plates (below).
. Click btn:[Print], or choose *Save as PDF* to hand the shop a file.

Each plate is a grayscale sheet: black where the ink prints, at the ink's percentage.
Registration marks and labels appear on every plate.  RGB colors and images are converted to
process inks through the document's CMYK working space; spot colors stay on their own plate
unless converted.

=== Who screens the plates

A halftone screen has to be applied by someone.  Two choices:

*Screen in {product}* off (the default)::
Plates are sent as continuous-tone grayscale.  The printer or RIP applies its own screen,
usually the one configured for the press, and the angles and frequencies in the ink list are
informational -- they are printed in the plate labels so the shop can match them.  This is the
right choice for any RIP-driven device.

*Screen in {product}* on::
{product} halftones each plate itself at the printer's resolution and sends one-bit plates.
The angles, frequencies and shapes in the ink list, and every object screen from the Halftones
panel (xref:halftones.adoc[Halftones]), are applied exactly.  Use it for a device with no
screening of its own, for effect screens that must survive intact, or to proof screening on a
desktop printer.  Jobs are large -- a letter-size plate at 2400 dpi is a 30-megapixel bitmap --
and take longer to spool.

Resolution comes from the printer queue; for *Save as PDF* it is the *Rasterize resolution*
value under *Imaging*, whether or not rasterizing is on.

== Working with a service provider

Send the shop what it asked for: a PDF/X export for a RIP, or the separated PDF from the Print
dialog, or the {product} document itself if the shop uses {product} (share it, rather than
sending a copy, so they always see the current version -- xref:sharing.adoc[Sharing]).  Send
along a document report (xref:file-info.adoc[Document info]) listing fonts, colors and placed
images, and check the fonts page for anything that will not embed
(xref:print-fonts.adoc[Printing fonts]).

Budget time for the round trip: proofs, corrections, and the shop's own schedule.

== Working with others

Output settings are shared with the document, so once one person has set the plates, marks
and bleed for a job, everyone prints it that way; a preset saved in the Print dialog records
the paper and printer choices that stay on each Mac.  Printing itself is always done by one
person on one Mac, with that Mac's fonts and printers.

== Specification

Separation is done by the renderer rather than by a PostScript interpreter.  Everything below
specifies that renderer mode and the in-app screener.

=== Data model

No fields beyond those on xref:printing.adoc[Printing]: `separations`, `plates`,
`spot_as_process`, `default_halftone`, `screen_in_app`, `rasterize_dpi`, `negative`,
`emulsion_down`.

=== Merge semantics

As on xref:printing.adoc[Printing].  Nothing here writes the document at print time.

=== Offline behavior

Fully offline.  Color conversion for separations uses the document's CMYK working space profile,
which is stored with the document or bundled with the app (CMS epic); no profile is fetched at
print time.

=== Server

Nothing.

=== Client

Plate mode in `WTRender`::
The display list is rendered once per plate with a `PlateContext` that maps every color
before it reaches Core Graphics:
* Each `Color` resolves to ink coverages `(c, m, y, k, spot[i])` -- process colors directly,
  spot colors as 100% of their own ink (tints as their percentage), RGB and Lab through the
  CMYK working space (CMS epic's conversion service).  With `spot_as_process` a spot resolves
  to its process alternate instead.
* On plate _P_ an object paints gray `1 - coverage(P)` in a `DeviceGray` context.  Coverage 0
  paints *white* (knockout) unless the fill or stroke is overprinting, in which case it paints
  nothing.  Blend mode is normal; the sheet starts white.
* Gradients map each stop; smooth shadings are emitted with mapped stops, so a C→M gradient on
  the cyan plate is a 0%→100% gray ramp.
* Images: CMYK images contribute their channel; RGB and gray images are converted through the
  working space per pixel (cached per image per plate); 1-bit images and masks are unchanged.
* Regions that the composite renderer rasterizes (transparency, raster effects, lens fills)
  are rasterized in CMYK through the working space and the plate channel extracted; overprint
  is not preserved inside such regions (documented limitation).
* Registration color (marks, labels) paints black on every plate.
* `negative` inverts the sheet with a final `difference` fill; `emulsion_down` flips the sheet
  transform horizontally.

In-app screener::
`WTInterchange/Print/Screener` rasterizes a plate at device resolution to 8-bit gray (tiled,
off the main actor), then thresholds per pixel against a spot function -- round, ellipse,
line, diamond, square, cross -- evaluated at the plate's angle and frequency, producing a 1-bit
`CGImage` drawn onto the sheet.  Objects with their own screen are rendered to separate gray
layers (one per distinct screen, in stacking order, each masked by its objects' coverage) and
thresholded with their screen before compositing; `ignore_object_halftones` collapses them.
Resolution: the queue's `NSPrintInfo` resolution, else `rasterize_dpi`.

Rasterize output::
The composite (or each plate) is rendered to an RGB (or gray) bitmap at `rasterize_dpi` and
drawn as one image per sheet.  Marks and labels remain vector.
